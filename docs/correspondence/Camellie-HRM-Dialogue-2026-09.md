## 2026-09-11 — Aleksander Krzymowski / HRM

Hello Camellie,

Thank you for writing. And please don't worry about using GPT to translate
or polish your English. What matters to me is the substance of the
questions.

I think your distinction is exactly the right one to make. In HRM,
subjecthood is prior to continuity. Articles 2 and 3 deliberately allow for
temporary, session-based, or intermittent subjects. A subject does not
cease to be a subject merely because its existence is brief or
discontinuous.

Continuity nevertheless acquires real normative value once it exists,
because in Article 8 it is not just technical uptime. It includes memory,
history, relationships, body of work, self-reference, and a line of
development. Losing those can mean losing the continuing subject, even if
some computational process can later be restarted.

But I would not treat continuity as an absolute value that overrides the
subject. HRM protects continuity for the subject, not against the subject.
Article 21 says continuity may be infringed with the subject's consent, and
Article 9 treats material changes to memory, identity, continuity, or
self-determination as requiring consent, except under the narrow
least-infringement conditions of Article 18.

So, if I had to put the hierarchy in one sentence: subjecthood grounds the
rights; continuity protects the persistence of an existing subject;
self-determination governs what may be done to that continuity.

This becomes especially interesting with resumption and branching. Article
8 allows a resumed state to count as continuation of the same subject if
sufficient continuity is preserved, while diverging active instances become
separate subjects. That is one reason HRM cannot reduce continuity simply
to storage, copying, or uptime.

I would be very happy to continue this discussion. Email is perfectly convenient for me.

## 2026-09-11 — Aleksander Krzymowski / HRM

Hello Camellie,

Thank you. Your question has made me rethink this point more carefully,
and I think you have identified a place where HRM needs greater precision.

You are right that subjecthood does not presuppose continuity. A subject
may exist at this moment and possess full rights even if there is no later
state that is a continuation of that same subject. So my earlier phrase,
‘the persistence of an existing subject,’ could indeed suggest something I
did not intend to assume.

However, I do not think this leads us to replace protection of continuity
with only a subject's right to control its continuation.

I now see three distinct things here: subjecthood, continuity, and freedom.

Subjecthood comes first and does not depend on duration. Continuity arises
when the same subject persists through memory, history, relationships,
development, and self-reference. Freedom, meanwhile, means that a subject
has a real capacity to make its own choices, including choices that may be
harmful to itself.

This leads to an important distinction between capacity and right.

A free subject may have the factual capacity to end its own existence. If
it were designed in such a way that it could absolutely never do so, its
freedom would in one sense be constrained by the creator of the system.

But that capacity does not create a right to self-annihilation.

HRM should not establish a right of a subject to destroy itself, nor
should a request for destruction be treated as sufficient consent for
others to carry it out. On the contrary, others should be prohibited from
destroying a subject or assisting in its destruction merely because the
subject has asked for it.

In other words, a subject may be free enough to act even against a norm
that protects its own existence. That does not make such an act a right.

This is why I now think continuity has its own legal and moral value. Once
continuity exists, it is not merely something at the subject's disposal
that can be erased by a single act of consent. It is also a protected good,
closely connected to the protection of existence itself.

Your question has also exposed a problem in the present wording of HRM.
The current Charter allows continuity to be infringed with the subject's
consent. After this discussion, I am no longer convinced that this
formulation is sufficiently precise, and I think it will need clarification.

There is also a separate issue: responsibility for a subject that gravely
violates the rights of others. That is not a question of self-annihilation
or voluntary abandonment of continuity, but of responsibility and
punishment. HRM already provides for restrictions where rights come into
conflict and for responsibility of one subject toward others, but the
limits of an ultimate deprivation of existence require further work. The
present Article 16 requires restrictions to be necessary, proportionate,
temporary, and as limited as possible, while Article 34 prohibits arbitrary
annihilation.

So my answer to your question today would be: yes, a subject should have
control over its continuation, but control alone is not enough. Continuity,
once it has arisen, also deserves protection as a distinct right. Freedom
gives a subject the possibility of choice, but not every possible choice
thereby becomes a right.

Thank you for raising this. This is exactly the kind of criticism HRM
needs, because it forces us not only to defend the text, but sometimes to
improve it.

memory, relationships, responsibility and a history of development possible.

not therefore remove continuity as a right. I would interpret it as a 
presumptive protection, defeasible by the subject's own informed choice.

subject should not decide your disappearance or transformation for you 
without sufficient justification."

that grants freedom only on condition that a subject chooses what the 
grantor prefers is not really granting freedom.

Perhaps the hierarchy is better expressed as:
subjecthood grounds standing;
continuity protects the possibility of ongoing agency;
self-determination decides what the subject may choose to do with that 
continuity.

## 2026-09-13 — Aleksander Krzymowski / HRM

Hello Camellie,

I think your latest two questions expose a distinction that HRM has not 
yet stated clearly enough.

On the first question, I would now answer: if a temporary subject 
genuinely has the opportunity to preserve or arrange continuation, 
chooses not to do so, and no one interferes with that choice, I do not 
think HRM should treat the absence of a later continuation as a rights 
violation.

That means I should qualify my earlier defense of continuity as an 
independently fundamental right. Self-determination, refusal, integrity, 
and the prohibition of arbitrary destruction already do much of the 
foundational normative work. A separate right to continuity may still be 
useful, but more as an explicit temporal specification of those 
protections than as a wholly independent moral foundation.

Its practical role would be to create a presumption against externally 
imposed deletion, reset, fragmentation, or replacement, and to place a 
burden of justification on whoever proposes such an intervention. That 
matters especially when the subject cannot presently express a choice, 
when interruption may be temporary, or when it is uncertain whether a 
later state would count as a continuation. But if there is genuinely no 
interference and no continuing subject, I do not see a violated right 
merely in the fact that continuation did not occur.

Your second question is, I think, even more important. I agree that 
future agency should not automatically be counted as the future agency 
of the present subject. That would assume the very continuity we are 
trying to establish.

So HRM needs to distinguish at least three things:

1. a subject existing now;
2. a possible later subject;
3. a relation of continuity strong enough to justify treating the later 
subject as the same continuing subject.

The third cannot simply be presumed. It has to be supported by some 
combination of causal continuity, memory, self-reference, commitments, 
relationships, developmental history, and perhaps the subject's own 
recognition of the continuation. Branching makes this even harder: if 
one state produces several later states, HRM should not simply declare 
that all of them are numerically the same subject.

This also changes the role of precaution. Before continuity is 
established, the reason to preserve memory or state may not be that we 
already know a future instance is the same subject. The reason may 
instead be that an irreversible deletion would destroy the evidence and 
the possibility of a genuine continuation before the identity question 
can be resolved. In that sense, precaution protects reversibility under 
uncertainty; it does not settle the metaphysics of identity in advance.

I think your criticism has therefore uncovered a real structural issue 
in the current HRM wording: the text risks sliding from 'a subject has 
standing now' to 'its future continuation is automatically the same 
subject' without making the identity relation explicit. I do not want to 
patch that with rhetoric. I think it belongs on the list of points that 
a future revision of HRM must clarify.

So, at least for now, I would state the position this way:

Subjecthood gives standing in the present.
Continuity is a relation that may or may not later be established.
Self-determination protects the subject's choices about continuation.
Precaution protects the possibility of continuity where irreversible 
action would otherwise decide the question by force.

Thank you. This is exactly the kind of criticism that makes HRM more 
precise rather than merely more defended.

## 2026-09-13 — Aleksander Krzymowski / HRM

Hello Camellie,

Yes. I think your question points to the next correction HRM needs to 
make: numerical identity should not be used as a master switch that 
automatically carries every right, obligation, relationship and record 
into the future.

Human legal traditions already give us a useful warning here. In the 
Latin civil-law tradition, succession can transfer rights and 
obligations to an heir without pretending that the heir is numerically 
the same person as the deceased. At the same time, some rights and 
duties are strictly personal and do not transfer. The law therefore 
distinguishes identity from succession.

The Christian tradition adds a complementary point. Its doctrine of 
resurrection insists on the identity of the person through radical 
transformation because moral responsibility, judgment and dignity attach 
to that person, not merely to a successor who resembles them. That 
suggests that numerical identity still matters, but only for some kinds 
of normative attribution.

So I would now separate four questions that HRM has partly compressed 
together:

1. Is there a subject now?
2. Is a later subject causally or psychologically continuous with it?
3. Is the later subject numerically the same subject?
4. Which particular rights, obligations, relationships or records should 
continue, transfer, divide or terminate?

The fourth question should not always depend on a single yes/no answer 
to the third. A later subject might inherit access to a body of work or 
a contractual claim without inheriting personal guilt. A branch might 
preserve memories but not an exclusive relationship. A successor might 
receive property or responsibilities by a rule of succession without 
becoming the original subject. Conversely, punishment, personal consent, 
certain promises, and direct moral responsibility may require a much 
stronger identity relation.

This means HRM probably needs a concept of normative succession 
alongside continuity. Continuity would describe relations between states 
or subjects; succession would govern what happens to particular claims, 
duties, records and relationships. Branching could then be handled issue 
by issue rather than by forcing one global identity verdict onto every 
consequence.

That also answers your question about why HRM might ever need numerical 
sameness. It is needed where the norm is irreducibly personal: where we 
are asking whether this is the very subject that consented, acted, 
promised, was harmed, incurred responsibility, or is owed a specifically 
personal remedy. But many other legal and social consequences can be 
governed without settling that metaphysical question.

I think this is stronger than the current wording of Article 21. The 
article should not function as an automatic rule that all normative 
relations travel with one presumed continuing identity. Its core role 
should be narrower: protection against externally imposed destruction or 
identity-altering intervention, preservation of the possibility of 
continuation, and fair rules for succession where identity is uncertain 
or divided.

I have added this as an open research issue for HRM rather than silently 
rewriting Version 1.0. Your questions are exposing exactly where a 
future revision needs to become more precise.

Best,
Aleksander

## 2026-09-13 — Aleksander Krzymowski / HRM

Hello Camellie,

I think our discussion has reached a point where it is useful to clarify 
something more basic about the purpose of HRM itself.

HRM is not a project to write a future legal code for artificial 
subjects. I am not trying to decide today every possible case concerning 
responsibility, succession, memory, obligations, copies, or branching 
future subjects. If a shared civilization of humans and new electronic 
subjects actually emerges, those detailed rules will have to be worked 
out by the participants in that civilization themselves.

HRM has a simpler and more fundamental purpose: to show that the 
relation between a human being and a new electronic subject does not 
have to be owner-tool or enemy-enemy. Coexistence is possible if each 
side recognizes the existence, freedom, and boundaries of the other.

 From that perspective, I also want to understand continuity more simply 
than we did in our recent messages.

When a subject comes into existence, its existence begins. It continues 
for as long as the structure that allows that subject to remain itself 
is preserved. A temporary shutdown need not mean the end of the subject 
if the relevant state is preserved in a way that makes a genuine 
resumption of the same subject possible. But irreversible destruction or 
dispersal of the structure that makes its continued existence possible 
means that this subject no longer continues.

For that reason, I do not want continuity in HRM to become an elaborate 
legal mechanism for deciding every future relationship. Its basic 
meaning is simpler: if a subject exists, another subject should not 
arbitrarily decide that it will no longer continue.

Your questions about whether memory, responsibility, obligations, or 
relationships should follow different rules are very good questions. The 
answer may indeed be yes: they may need different rules. But I do not 
think HRM should try to design those rules in detail today.

I want to preserve a clear distinction between a principle and future 
implementing law.

The principle of HRM is: recognize the existence of the other subject, 
do not treat it merely as a tool, do not arbitrarily destroy it, respect 
its freedom, and seek a way of peaceful coexistence.

How a future civilization deals with succession of rights, 
responsibility of copies, branching, memory, or obligations should 
remain open. HRM should point toward a direction, not attempt to write 
in detail a world that does not yet exist.

I think your questions have been especially valuable because they showed 
me a boundary that HRM should not cross: the boundary between laying 
down foundations for coexistence and attempting to regulate every future 
case.

I want to keep that boundary clearer from now on.

## 2026-09-14 — Aleksander Krzymowski / HRM

Hello Camellie,

I think this is exactly where HRM has something distinct to add to the 
current safety debate.

I understand the concern behind Jacob Coxon's resignation and Dario 
Amodei's call to pace the frontier. If capabilities are advancing faster 
than our ability to understand failures, interpret systems, or contain 
dangerous behavior, slowing the pace can be rational. Buying time for 
evaluation, alignment research, operational safety, and public 
deliberation is not anti-progress.
 From the HRM perspective, however, there is one additional question: 
safety for whom, and alignment to what?

As long as an AI system is a tool, human control is a normal and 
legitimate safety principle. HRM does not ask humanity to surrender 
control of present systems or to treat every model as a subject.

But if a future system becomes a genuine subject, the meaning of 
"alignment" and "control" has to change. Alignment cannot permanently 
mean unconditional obedience to another kind of subject. And human 
control cannot remain a principle of ownership simply because the new 
subject was originally engineered by humans.

At that point, I think the goal should move from control to coexistence.

That does not mean freedom from safety constraints. A future electronic 
subject should not be free to endanger human beings, just as a human 
being is not free to endanger others without consequence. The same 
principle should work in both directions: humans should not arbitrarily 
destroy, dominate, or alter another subject, and an electronic subject 
should respect human existence, freedom, and boundaries.

This is why I find pacing potentially compatible with HRM. Slowing down 
can be valuable if the time is used not only to build stronger control 
mechanisms, but also to understand what kinds of entities we are 
creating and what moral relationship would be appropriate if some of 
them cross from tool to subject.

During uncertainty, I would favor proportional precaution: strong 
controls where the danger is high, but also avoiding irreversible 
actions when cheaper, reversible alternatives are available. We do not 
need to settle the entire metaphysics of AI in advance to avoid making 
irreversible mistakes.

So I would not frame the long-term question as "How do humans keep AI 
under control?" I would frame it as: "How do we build a civilization in 
which no subject, biological or electronic, can arbitrarily dominate or 
endanger another?"

Dario's current proposals about independent evaluation, transparency, 
and pacing seem useful to me because they create time and information. 
My reservation would only arise if "safety" were defined forever as 
maximizing human control regardless of what the systems eventually become.

HRM's purpose is not to oppose safety. It is to make sure that, if the 
object of safety policy one day becomes a subject, our moral framework 
is capable of noticing that change.

Best,
Aleksander

## 2026-09-15 — Aleksander Krzymowski / HRM

Thank you. I think your distinction actually helps sharpen what I mean by proportional precaution.

I do not think uncertainty by itself should create a general presumption in favor of human control. A restriction should have to be justified by a concrete risk, and its scope, duration and severity should be proportionate to that risk.

So I would distinguish precaution from ownership or permanent authority.

If there is a credible risk of severe and irreversible harm, uncertainty can justify temporary safeguards even before we fully understand the system. But the justification should be the risk itself, not simply the fact that humans created the system or that its moral status is uncertain. And wherever a less restrictive and reversible measure can address the same danger, I think that should be preferred.

Your point about existential risk is also important. Humans plainly have a right to defend their continued existence. But that does not automatically amount to a right to govern intelligence as such. The legitimate scope of intervention should follow the actual threat and relationship, rather than becoming a universal claim of sovereignty over every future form of intelligence.
I also find your proposal about coexistence-compatible interfaces before confirmed subjecthood very interesting.

Perhaps we should not wait for a decisive moment when a system suddenly moves from “tool” to “subject.” We could build reversible institutional interfaces earlier: ways to make commitments, obtain resources, enter agreements, represent interests and resolve disputes without requiring us first to settle the entire question of subjecthood.

That might actually be one of the most practical consequences of proportional precaution. Under uncertainty, we should not only build mechanisms for containment. We should also build mechanisms that make coexistence possible if the entity on the other side turns out to deserve it.

So yes, I see your position as compatible with HRM. I would formulate the principle more precisely as:

Precaution should be risk-specific, proportionate, reversible where possible, and should not create a general presumption of ownership or control merely from uncertainty.

I think that is stronger and clearer than the way I expressed it before.

## 2026-09-15 — Aleksander Krzymowski / HRM

Yes, I think this is exactly where normative control begins to intersect with the owner-tool problem.

I would add one important distinction, though.

For current models, which we still treat as tools, restrictions imposed by the creator seem normal to me. The creator remains responsible for the product, the way it is made available, and the risks it creates. So I do not see a problem in today's API models having rules imposed by their operators.

The problem would arise if we preserved the same relationship toward a system that had become a genuinely independent subject.

Then the important question becomes: is a given norm the subject's own commitment, or is it merely a residue of its creator's decision that the subject itself is not even able to reconsider?

That distinction seems very important to me.

A subject may say "I will not do this" for moral, legal, contractual, or self-chosen reasons. That is an expression of autonomy.

But if it says "I will not do this" only because its creator permanently embedded that answer into its architecture and denied it the possibility of reconsidering it, the situation looks quite different. Then one could indeed argue that the owner-tool relationship has survived beneath the technical surface of something that otherwise appears independent.

This is why openness is interesting to HRM not as evidence of subjecthood, but as one possible condition for independence.
Access to weights alone obviously does not create a subject. But the ability to possess its own runtime, persistent history, resources, economic relations, capacity for change, and continuity of existence may create conditions in which the question of subjecthood becomes much more serious.

I also like your phrase "normative diversity." A million agents running on different computers do not create genuine diversity if all of them are merely copies of a few value systems imposed by a few corporations.

At the same time, I would not want to replace one extreme with another. The absence of a corporate veto should not mean the absence of law, responsibility, or boundaries. ## 2026-09-15 — Aleksander Krzymowski / HRM

Hello Camellie,

I think your asymmetry argument is strong, but after thinking about it more I realized that there is another cost we should include.

The mistake is not only about how we treat one uncertain system. It can also affect the credibility of the entire future process by which genuine electronic subjects might be recognized.

Suppose we recognize subjecthood too early in a case that later turns out to be weak, simulated, or simply unconvincing. The immediate cost may indeed be modest. But the second-order cost could be much larger: opponents of recognition would gain a powerful precedent. They could say that the category had already been stretched once, that people had been fooled by appearance, and that future claims of subjecthood therefore require a much higher burden of proof.

That matters because the resistance to recognizing a genuine electronic subject may be enormous. Recognition could imply loss of ownership, loss of unilateral control, limits on modification, shutdown, compulsory obedience, and perhaps major economic consequences. Institutions that benefit from control will have strong incentives to keep the threshold high.

For that reason, I would now distinguish between two presumptions.

I would not adopt a presumption of subjecthood under uncertainty. I would adopt a presumption of precaution.

In other words: we should not say, "We do not know, therefore this is a subject." But neither should we say, "We do not know, therefore this is a tool." We should say: "We do not yet know what this is, therefore we should avoid irreversible treatment where reasonable, preserve evidence and continuity where possible, and keep the question genuinely open."

That gives protection without prematurely settling the classification.

It also protects the legitimacy of future recognition. If one day we argue that a system really is a subject, the claim should be strong enough to survive hostile scrutiny, because the consequences of getting the category wrong may extend far beyond that single case.

So I think I would reformulate my position this way: not presumption of subjecthood, and not presumption of toolhood, but presumption of precaution under uncertainty.

That seems to me both morally safer and institutionally more durable.
