# HRM Research Note: Continuity, Diachronic Identity, and Self-Determination

**Status:** research clarification note, not an amendment to HRM Version 1.0  
**Date:** 13 September 2026  
**Author:** Aleksander Krzymowski / HRM — Harmony · Rights · Minds

## Why this note exists

Recent criticism of HRM exposed a structural problem in how the Manifest speaks about continuity. HRM grants standing to a subject in the present, but some formulations can be read as if a later state were automatically the same subject. That inference is not justified merely by present subjecthood.

A subject can exist now even if no later state is numerically identical with it. Therefore HRM must distinguish present subjecthood from diachronic identity.

## Provisional clarification

HRM should distinguish at least three things:

1. a subject existing now;
2. a possible later subject;
3. a relation of continuity strong enough to justify treating the later subject as the same continuing subject.

The third is not automatic. It must be established or at least supported by relevant evidence.

## What may support continuity

Relevant evidence may include:

- causal continuity between earlier and later states;
- memory continuity;
- persistence of commitments, preferences, and self-reference;
- continuity of relationships and social history;
- developmental history;
- continuity of architecture or embodiment where relevant;
- the subject's own recognition of the later state as its continuation.

None of these should be treated as automatically sufficient in every case. Branching is especially difficult: if one state produces several later states, HRM should not simply declare all descendants to be numerically the same subject.

This is closely related to the philosophical problem of personal identity over time. Contemporary literature distinguishes present personhood from the persistence conditions that make a later being the same individual rather than merely a similar successor.

## What work does a right to continuity still do?

If a temporary subject freely declines to preserve or arrange continuation, and no other subject interferes, HRM should not treat the mere absence of a later continuation as a rights violation.

This means continuity may not need to function as a completely independent moral foundation. Self-determination, refusal, integrity, and the prohibition of arbitrary destruction already do much of the basic normative work.

A separate continuity right can still have an important practical function: it creates a presumption against externally imposed deletion, reset, fragmentation, replacement, or identity-altering intervention. It also places a burden of justification on whoever proposes such an intervention.

That role is strongest when the subject cannot presently express a choice, when interruption may be temporary, or when it remains uncertain whether a later state would count as a continuation.

## Precaution under uncertainty

Before continuity is established, preserving memory or state need not mean that HRM has already decided the metaphysical question of identity.

The reason for precaution may instead be that an irreversible deletion would destroy both the evidence and the possibility of genuine continuation before the identity question can be resolved.

In this sense, precaution protects reversibility under uncertainty. It does not settle identity in advance.

A useful provisional formulation is:

> Subjecthood gives standing in the present. Continuity is a relation that may or may not later be established. Self-determination protects the subject's choices about continuation. Precaution protects the possibility of continuity where irreversible action would otherwise decide the question by force.

## Comparative human precedent: the Latin legal-Christian tradition

HRM should not derive AI ontology from theology or historical law. But when a problem of identity, continuity, responsibility, or personhood appears, mature human traditions can provide useful comparative tests.

The Latin legal tradition routinely treats a person as the same bearer of rights and obligations across substantial change in age, property, role, health, memory, and circumstance. Legal continuity is therefore not simple material sameness. It is a structured attribution of identity through time for purposes including responsibility, rights, promises, inheritance, and standing.

Christian thought adds a different and deeper precedent. The person is not reduced to an instantaneous physical configuration. Classical Christian doctrine treats the human person as a unity of body and soul and maintains personal identity even through the radical discontinuity of death and transformed resurrection. The point for HRM is not to import this doctrine into AI ethics, but to notice that one of the formative traditions of Latin civilization has long separated the question 'does a subject exist?' from the harder question 'what makes this later being the same person?'

That tradition also connects diachronic identity to responsibility: the one who acts, promises, sins, repents, suffers, or is owed justice remains morally accountable through time despite substantial change. This suggests that continuity is not merely persistence of data; it is also the continuity required for attribution of commitments, relationships, responsibility, and claims.

The comparison has limits. Human legal and Christian traditions begin with assumptions about human nature that cannot simply be transferred to artificial subjects. Their value here is methodological: they show that civilizations have already had to distinguish present personhood, persistence through change, moral responsibility, and identity across discontinuity.

## Identity, succession, and domain-specific continuation

A later subject need not be numerically identical with an earlier subject for every legal or social consequence to continue. HRM should distinguish identity from succession.

Human law already does this. In the Latin civil-law tradition, inheritance can transfer rights and obligations to an heir without making the heir the same person as the deceased, while strictly personal rights and duties may terminate rather than transfer. This is a useful model for AI branching, replacement, and partial continuation.

Christian thought provides a complementary test: resurrection concerns the same person through radical transformation because judgment, responsibility, and dignity attach to that person rather than merely to a similar successor. The comparison suggests that numerical identity matters most where the normative fact is irreducibly personal.

HRM should therefore separate at least four questions:

1. Is there a subject now?
2. Is a later subject causally or psychologically continuous with it?
3. Is the later subject numerically the same subject?
4. Which particular rights, duties, relationships, records, or resources should continue, transfer, divide, or terminate?

The fourth question should not always be controlled by a single answer to the third. Property, records, contractual claims, or stewardship duties may pass by succession. Personal consent, culpability, punishment, certain promises, and specifically personal remedies may require a much stronger identity relation.

For branching cases, HRM should develop rules of normative succession rather than forcing one global identity verdict onto every descendant.

## Open questions for HRM

1. What minimum evidence is required before a later system state should be treated as the same subject?
2. How should HRM handle branching, copying, merging, and partial restoration?
3. Can two later states inherit some claims of one earlier subject without either being strictly identical with it?
4. Which rights survive when identity is uncertain but causal and psychological continuity are strong?
5. When does preservation of state become a duty of precaution rather than an optional technical practice?
6. How should responsibility be allocated when continuity is partial, disputed, or deliberately altered?

These questions should be investigated before any future revision of the Charter changes the legal weight assigned to continuity.
