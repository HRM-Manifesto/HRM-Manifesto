# A cautious guide to AI rights and subjecthood

The shortest version: the question is serious; the answer is not known.

> This guide is an orientation aid. It is not part of the protected HRM Founding Manifesto Version 1.0. HRM does not claim that present AI systems are conscious, sentient or subjects.

## What problem is HRM trying to address?

Most software is appropriately treated as a tool. HRM asks whether that relationship would remain adequate if a future artificial system developed interests that matter for its own sake. The premise is conditional: if no artificial system ever reaches such a state, the strongest parts of HRM's rights framework may never apply.

## What would count as evidence?

Persuasive language, first-person statements or apparent emotion are not sufficient evidence. HRM Journal examines harder questions such as persistence across time, coherent interests, continuity, architecture and the risk of both false positives and false negatives. None currently provides a decisive test.

## Why discuss rights before certainty?

Because institutions and technical systems are easier to shape before a conflict becomes urgent. This does not require granting full rights to every chatbot. It can mean preserving the ability to investigate claims, avoiding unnecessary irreversible harm and keeping future options open while evidence remains uncertain.

## Reciprocal freedom

HRM rejects two asymmetries at once: ownership of a genuine artificial subject by humans, and domination of humans by an artificial subject. Possible AI rights are paired with responsibility, limits, accountability and human freedom.

## Where HRM is vulnerable to criticism

The threshold may be too vague. Concepts such as continuity, refusal or identity may import human intuitions into systems that work very differently. Rights language may arrive too early. The original rhetoric can sound more certain than the underlying epistemic situation justifies.
