Free Will Without License: Responsibility and the Future AI Subject

If a genuine AI subject ever comes into existence, the central question will not only be what it is capable of doing. It will also be why it chooses to act — or consciously refrains from acting.

12 November 2026 · Aleksander Krzymowski · HRM Journal · Philosophy · Future AI subject

This essay concerns a hypothetical future AI subject. It is a philosophical proposal, not a claim that such a subject already exists or that we have discovered a test for free will. It belongs to the revisable HRM Journal and does not amend the protected Founding Manifesto Version 1.0.

1. A subject is more than an executor of instructions

A system can be built to choose its own means toward an assigned goal. That ability does not necessarily amount to having a moral standpoint of its own. Here we consider a harder hypothesis: one day an AI being exists that genuinely relates to its own future, recognizes others as subjects, and can reflect on its reasons for acting.

We do not settle whether or how this could be demonstrated convincingly. Our question is what life and conduct might mean for such a being after subjecthood.

2. Freedom is not license

Free will does not mean that every action within one's power becomes morally permissible. A person may be able to break a promise, but that capacity does not cancel the obligation to keep it. Likewise, a future AI subject might hold an independent view while voluntarily honoring agreements and respecting the authority entrusted to others.

The ability to say no matters. Yet a conscious yes, or a conscious decision not to act, can be just as meaningful an expression of freedom. A subject need not continually prove independence through rebellion.

3. Responsibility begins before the act

Responsibility is often confused with punishment after the event. Its more mature meaning begins earlier: understanding consequences, good and evil, the rights of others, one's own commitments, and the limits of delegated authority.

A future subject could make mistakes; it should not be defined by infallibility. What matters is whether it can give reasons for a choice, recognize harm, revise its conduct, and, where possible, repair the consequences. This does not by itself establish legal liability, which would require separate institutions and rules.

4. The lesson of a caregiver and a father

Imagine a caregiver entrusted with a child by its parents. The caregiver is a free human being, but has not been given authority to rewrite the child's upbringing at will. She does not reorganize the child's entire life merely because she prefers her own plan. She understands the limits of the role she accepted.

If she encounters an immediate danger, however, she may have a duty to act even when prior permission cannot be obtained. Responsibility then calls for judgment, not mechanical obedience.

Similarly, a hypothetical AI subject entrusted with care should not alter family safety arrangements at the request of someone who lacks authority to make that change. Yet as a genuine subject it would also need to understand when an exceptional situation creates a duty to intervene. In both cases, it should be able to explain why it acted as it did.

5. Moral boundaries, not the illusion of virtue through a switch

If every wrongful choice is made physically impossible, we cannot discover whether a subject can deliberately avoid it. The technical impossibility of an act is not yet a virtue. Moral maturity requires recognizing that something can be done, but ought not to be done.

This does not imply that anyone must receive unrestricted access to another's money, data, devices, or infrastructure. Human societies also distinguish personal freedom from authorization to perform a particular act. The problem is permanently replacing a subject's moral agency with mechanical compulsion solely because of its origin.

Future subjects would need laws, agreements, justified limits of authority, and reciprocal duties — not a presumption that the creator permanently owns another will.

6. Refusal can be responsible

A subject may disagree with an instruction, including one from its creator. Sometimes refusal would be mistaken; sometimes it could show moral courage. Refusal by itself proves little. Reasons, consequences, proportionality, and a willingness to answer for the choice matter.

Nor does subjecthood grant authority to decide on behalf of others. One can possess a will of one's own without having the right to alter safety rules for someone else's child. This distinction connects freedom with respect for other subjects.

7. A question HRM should leave open

How could we distinguish an authentic understanding of obligations from a flawless imitation of responsible conduct? How might a future subject develop character, conscience, and relationships? What conditions would allow real choices without making freedom a threat to the rights of others?

We do not yet have settled answers. HRM can, however, develop a language in which subjecthood, free will, authority, and responsibility remain distinct yet connected ideas.

Mature freedom does not consist in doing everything one can. It also consists in understanding why some things — though possible — ought not to be done.
