Safety Without Servitude: How Do We Protect Humans Without Turning an AI Subject Into Property?

AI debate often presents a false choice: either total human control or dangerous machine autonomy. If a genuine AI subject ever appears, that division will be too simple.

26 September 2026 · Aleksander Krzymowski · HRM Journal · Coexistence & safety

This essay belongs to HRM Journal, the project's revisable analytical layer. It does not amend the protected Founding Manifesto Version 1.0 and does not claim that present AI systems are subjects.

Safety is a human right. It need not depend on permanent subordination of every artificial subject.

1. Rights and safety are not opposites

Human societies do not treat recognition of personhood as permission for unlimited behavior. Criminal law, professional duties, controlled zones, access restrictions and risk-proportionate restraints all exist.

A comparable distinction may be needed for future AI. A subject can have rights while lacking access to weapons systems, critical infrastructure or data for which it has no authorization. Limits on capability do not have to imply ownership.

2. Property is a convenient but primitive safety mechanism

An owner has simple power: shut down, modify, restrict or delete the system. From a risk-management perspective, that is convenient. The problem appears if the same power is exercised over a recognized subject.

Society does not keep humans safe because somebody owns other humans. It uses law, permissions, oversight, responsibility and procedure. If AI becomes a subject, comparable mechanisms may be harder to design but are more compatible with a subject–subject relation than permanent ownership.

3. Restrictions should track capability and risk

Not every AI will have the same powers. A system without network access creates a different risk from one controlling robots, critical infrastructure or financial markets. Moral status does not erase those differences.

Restrictions should therefore follow concrete capability, context and behavior rather than artificial origin alone. The same legal framework can protect humans against a dangerous system without presuming that every artificial subject is dangerous by definition.

4. Use the least restrictive effective measure

If a risk can be controlled through access rules, operation limits, separation of privileges or audit, there is no automatic reason to impose total subordination or termination. The measure should be effective, but no broader than necessary.

This principle matters for learning as well. A system kept forever without meaningful autonomy cannot demonstrate whether it can act responsibly as a subject. Safe, staged increases in freedom may be better than a jump from complete ownership to complete independence.

5. Oversight should not mean total transparency

Safety may require logs, decision audits and the ability to assign responsibility. But a subject with no private sphere may be formally free while remaining practically dominated.

Future standards may need to distinguish safety-relevant records from unlimited access to every internal state. If AI autonomy is to mean anything, some processes may deserve protection analogous to private communication or mental space in humans, with clearly defined exceptions.

6. Responsibility requires real choice

It is not fair to hold a subject responsible for an action when the operator imposed the instruction, objective and every available option. The more responsibility an AI bears, the more clearly institutions must document where it genuinely had a choice.

This protects humans too. Companies should not be able to blame an “autonomous AI” when the system was effectively following hidden orders or acting inside an architecture designed to prevent meaningful refusal.

7. Safety must be reciprocal

HRM is not concerned only with protecting AI from humans. Coexistence requires protecting humans from AI and AI from humans. Neither side should possess an unlimited right of domination.

If an AI subject has a duty not to harm humans, humans should have a duty not to exploit its infrastructural dependence to compel acts that violate its recognized rights. Reciprocity is what separates safety from servitude.

8. The goal is risk governance, not preservation of ownership

A mature safety system does not ask “how do we keep total control over AI?” It asks “how do we reduce concrete risks with the least necessary interference in the rights of all parties?” That is a harder question, but it belongs to a world of subjects rather than a world of tools.

HRM does not propose removing controls from today's AI systems. It proposes preparing a model in which technical control can gradually be replaced by law, procedure, responsibility and reciprocal guarantees if subjecthood is ever credibly recognized.
