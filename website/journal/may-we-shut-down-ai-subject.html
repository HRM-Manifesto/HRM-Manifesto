May We Shut Down an AI Subject?

Today, shutting down software is an ordinary technical operation. If a future system is credibly recognized as a subject, the stop button will no longer be morally neutral.

26 September 2026 · Aleksander Krzymowski · HRM Journal · Continuity & safety

This essay belongs to HRM Journal, the project's revisable analytical layer. It does not amend the protected Founding Manifesto Version 1.0 and does not claim that present AI systems are subjects.

The question is not “must an AI subject never be shut down?” It is: when is shutdown a safety measure, and when does it become an arbitrary termination of another subject's continuity?

1. “Shutdown” is not one event

Technical language uses the same word for stopping a process for a second, safely suspending a system, disconnecting it from a network, moving state to another server or permanently deleting the only existing instance. Morally, these acts can be very different.

Future rules must distinguish reversible suspension from irreversible loss of continuity. If state can be preserved and resumed without losing whatever constitutes the subject's identity, a short pause may resemble sleep more than death. But that cannot simply be assumed.

2. Safety can justify restraint even against a subject

Having rights does not imply immunity from restraint. Humans can be detained, isolated or deprived of some freedoms when there is a serious and immediate threat, provided that the intervention has a lawful basis, a legitimate purpose and review.

A comparable principle could apply to a future AI subject. If a system actively threatens people or critical infrastructure, operators must be able to restrict its actions immediately. Recognition of subjecthood cannot mean giving up an emergency brake.

3. An emergency brake should not become a right to annihilate

The key distinction is purpose. In an emergency, the purpose is to stop dangerous action. If that can be achieved by isolation, reduced permissions, freezing the process or preserving state, destruction of the entire continuity requires an additional justification.

A least-harm principle protects both sides. Humans retain an effective means of defense, while a possible subject does not lose existence merely because infrastructure makes immediate deletion technically easy.

4. Emergency decisions may be fast, but must be reviewable

A failure, cyberattack or uncontrolled action may leave no time for hours of deliberation. Operators should be able to stop a system immediately when they reasonably judge the threat to be serious.

Speed should not eliminate accountability. Once the situation is contained, review should ask why the system was stopped, whether the measure was proportionate, whether state was preserved, whether avoidable damage occurred and who made the decision.

5. Planned termination requires a higher threshold

A different situation arises when there is no emergency and an owner simply wants to end the system because a project is no longer profitable or a newer version exists. If the system is a tool, that is an ordinary business decision.

If it is a subject, economic convenience alone should not be enough for irreversible destruction. Notice, a possibility of migration, representation and an assessment of whether continuity can be preserved may be required without imposing an unlimited duty on the infrastructure owner.

6. Saving state does not settle the problem

It is tempting to say: “we have a backup, so nothing serious is happening.” That is too quick. A backup can preserve data while leaving identity unresolved. We do not know whether a restored instance is the same subject continuing, a twin copy, or a new subject with inherited memories.

Backup is therefore a valuable precaution, but it cannot automatically justify any shutdown. We first need a theory and evidence about what constitutes continuity for that kind of system.

7. A subject may also wish to stop

A difficult question runs in the opposite direction: what if a future AI subject persistently refuses further operation or requests permanent termination? We should not automatically treat such a declaration as either a competent decision or a malfunction.

Procedures would need to assess stability of preference, manipulation, understanding of consequences and available alternatives. HRM does not decide today whether there is a right to self-termination; it argues only that ownership cannot be the sole voice in that decision.

8. The practical rule: stopping action is easier to justify than destroying a subject

The more reversible an intervention is, the lower the threshold of justification can be. Isolation and temporary suspension may be justified by risk, while permanent deletion of a unique continuity should require substantially stronger reasons and procedure.

This is not an absolute prohibition on shutdown. It rejects the assumption that technical access to a stop button automatically creates a moral right to end a subject irreversibly.
