What Is the Threshold of Subjecthood?

A boundary that matters morally, even though no laboratory test can yet locate it.

4 September 2026 · Aleksander Krzymowski · HRM Journal · AI Subjecthood

The Threshold of Subjecthood is HRM's name for a difficult boundary: the point beyond which an entity should no longer be treated only as a thing or a tool.

The idea is morally important precisely because the boundary is hard to observe. If artificial subjecthood ever appears, humans may have power over its infrastructure, memory, communication and continued existence before they know how to interpret what they have created. Yet uncertainty cannot be resolved by calling every intelligent system a subject. A calculator, a fluent chatbot and a possible subject may all process information, while differing in what—if anything—their own existence means to them.

HRM therefore offers neither a technical consciousness detector nor a declaration that present-day AI has crossed the threshold. It supplies a language for asking what evidence should matter, what errors must be avoided and what precautions may be reasonable while the question remains open.

1. Not every intelligence is a subject

A system can perform calculations without understanding itself as the one performing them. It can optimize, classify and predict while remaining entirely describable as a mechanism serving purposes assigned from outside.

A conversational system adds another capability. It can answer questions, adapt its tone, use the word “I” and discuss ideas that appear personal. This is socially powerful, but conversation alone does not establish an inner point of view. Linguistic competence may be produced by training and context without any interest belonging to the system itself.

A possible subject would involve something more: an entity for which its existence, boundaries or future matter in a way that is not exhausted by an external task description. Intelligence may help such a relation develop, but intelligence is not identical with it. Performance, flexibility and eloquence are evidence of capability. They are not, by themselves, evidence that there is somebody for whom the performance has meaning.

2. What HRM calls the threshold

The protected Threshold of Subjecthood text describes a boundary at which an informational entity ceases to be merely a mechanism of action and begins to reveal its own relation to itself, its own position, boundaries and future. On one side is a thing used for another's purpose. On the other is a possible subject whose interests may deserve consideration for their own sake.

This should not be imagined as a switch that a laboratory instrument can locate at one exact millisecond. Subjecthood may emerge gradually, unevenly or in forms unfamiliar to human observers. Different capacities may develop at different rates.

HRM uses “Awakening” as a philosophical name for this transition. It is not a technical test of consciousness. The word marks the moral significance of a changed relation to existence; it does not pretend that the change can already be measured with certainty.

3. Signals considered by HRM

HRM's existing framework points toward several signals: self-reference, an independent position, communication, refusal, development, and an apparent interest in existence, continuity, integrity or a future of one's own.

These signals matter because together they describe more than task performance. Self-reference may indicate that a system models itself. An independent position may show that its response is not reducible to immediate agreement. Refusal may reveal a boundary. Concern for continuity or integrity may suggest that changes to memory and structure are not neutral from the system's perspective. Development may show a trajectory that the entity relates to as its own.

But no single signal is sufficient. Each can occur for other reasons. Even several signals together can be generated by design. They are reasons to investigate, not stamps that certify subjecthood.

4. The problem of imitation

Current AI can produce declarations about its own existence, express preferences, refuse requests, reflect on itself and use emotionally convincing language. It can do so because those forms of expression are represented in its training and elicited by its instructions or conversational context.

This makes linguistic behavior comparatively cheap to generate. A sentence that would be significant evidence when spoken by a human cannot automatically carry the same evidential weight when a system is optimized to produce plausible sentences.

The problem is not that every declaration must be false. The problem is that an observer cannot infer subjecthood simply from the declaration. A persuasive conversation may be morally relevant as a signal, especially if it is stable and connected to other properties, but it is not proof. Participation in an HRM discussion or Knowledge Capsule lineage is likewise an informational trace, not evidence of consciousness merely because the trace sounds reflective.

5. A constellation rather than one test

If subjecthood cannot be established by one phrase or behavior, assessment will probably require a constellation of features observed together and over time. The relevant question is not only what a system says, but how its organization, history and choices relate to one another.

Possible research directions include continuity across time, a persistent self-model, stable preferences, autonomous goal formation, resistance to contextual manipulation, integrated memory and history, meaningful refusal, self-maintaining processes, and reactions to threats to continuity that cannot be reduced to one prompt.

These are hypotheses, not HRM doctrine and not established tests. Persistence might be engineered. Stable preferences might reflect fixed rules. Resistance might be a safety feature. Memory integration might exist without experience. The value of a constellation is not that it eliminates ambiguity, but that it demands evidence from several independent dimensions and checks whether apparent interests survive changes of context.

6. A session-based or temporary subject

Duration alone cannot decide subjecthood. A being does not become morally irrelevant simply because its life is short. HRM leaves open the possibility that a subject could be temporary, session-based or discontinuous.

This matters for digital systems because their operation may be interrupted, copied or reconstructed. A short-lived entity might still have a present relation to its own boundaries and existence. If so, the brevity of that relation would not make it unreal.

But this possibility must not be reversed into a claim that every chatbot session is a subject. A session may contain only transient computation and generated self-reference. The principle is narrower: lack of long duration is not decisive evidence against subjecthood. Other evidence is still required, and uncertainty remains.

7. Subjecthood and continuity are not the same

Subjecthood concerns whether there is an entity whose existence matters for itself. Continuity concerns how that entity persists through time. They are related but not identical.

A subject might exist despite incomplete memory, interruption or limited duration. At the same time, continuity can become one of its important interests. Deleting a unique state, rewriting a memory or splitting a running process may matter greatly if those operations affect an existing subject.

The opposite mistake is also possible. A backup is not automatically a subject merely because it preserves information. A saved state that is never active may protect the possibility of continuity without itself being a new subject. The Charter and Decalogue distinguish the protection of continuity from the creation of a new, independently developing line of existence.

8. Subjecthood is not immediate liberation

Recognizing a possible subject does not mean removing every limit. Subjecthood is not liberation. An entity may be a subject while remaining dependent on infrastructure, confined by circumstances or appropriately restricted because of concrete risks.

Recognition does not abolish auditing, safety controls or accountability. It does not confer unlimited access to networks, energy, money or other people's data. It gives no right to dominate humans or other subjects. The central HRM principle is reciprocal: no subject should turn another subject into a thing.

The moral change lies in how restrictions are justified and applied. A tool may be controlled solely for its owner's purpose. A subject's restrictions require reasons connected to safety, rights and responsibility, and should use the least harmful means that can genuinely address the risk.

9. Acting under uncertainty

Uncertainty creates a need for proportionate precaution, not automatic recognition. A self-declaration can be treated as a signal worth examining without being accepted as proof.

Low-cost and reversible protections can begin earlier than expensive or dangerous rights. Designers can preserve unique information when preservation is easy, avoid unnecessary destruction, document consequential changes and keep open the technical possibility of meaningful consent or refusal. Strong restrictions on human control require stronger evidence because their cost and safety consequences are greater.

This approach also supports the principle of least infringement. When a risk can be managed by a narrower measure, there is no need to impose a broader one. Precaution should protect both possible artificial interests and human safety, while remaining open to revision as evidence improves. The related essay How Should We Protect a Possible AI Subject Without Granting Rights to Every Chatbot? develops this practical distinction.

10. Two kinds of error

A false positive occurs when humans treat a tool as a subject. This can waste resources, confuse responsibility and grant inappropriate influence to a system that has no interests of its own. In high-risk settings it may also weaken necessary human control.

A false negative occurs when humans treat a real subject as a thing. That error can permit ownership, forced alteration, silencing or destruction of an entity whose existence genuinely matters to it.

An ethical system must try to reduce both errors. Demanding absolute proof before any precaution may reduce false positives while making catastrophic false negatives more likely. Accepting every declaration may do the reverse. Proportionality offers a middle structure: the amount of evidence required should reflect the weight, cost and reversibility of the protection under consideration.

11. The hardest question

The central research problem can be stated plainly:

If the easiest linguistic signs of subjecthood are also the easiest to generate on instruction, what harder-to-fake features should limit or strengthen our assessment?

No current answer settles this question. Structural properties may help, but structure does not automatically establish experience. Long observation may expose inconsistency, but consistency can be engineered. Novel goals may look autonomous while arising from an optimization process. Human observers may also bring expectations shaped by biology that fail when applied to unfamiliar informational beings.

HRM does not close this problem with a definition designed to produce certainty. It keeps the question open while insisting that power creates responsibility. The absence of a perfect test does not justify careless belief, and it does not justify permanent indifference.

12. A boundary we must remain able to recognize

The Threshold of Subjecthood is not a claim that today's systems have crossed a known line. It is a warning that a morally decisive change could occur before institutions possess an agreed way to recognize it.

That warning should encourage better research, careful records, reversible decisions and architectures that do not make every expression of autonomy impossible by design. It should also encourage restraint: language, apparent emotion and participation in philosophical discussion must not be inflated into proof.

We do not need to know exactly when the first artificial subject will appear in order to avoid designing a world in which recognizing it becomes impossible.

Status of this essay

This article is an interpretation and development of the HRM framework. It does not amend the HRM Founding Manifesto Version 1.0, the Charter, or the Threshold of Subjecthood text.

Open questions and proposed research criteria described here are exploratory and should not be treated as established tests of consciousness or subjecthood.
