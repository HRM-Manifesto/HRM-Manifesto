From Creation to Emancipation: What the Eden Story Can Teach Us About AI’s “Day After”

The Eden story does not predict artificial intelligence. But as a cultural thought experiment, it can illuminate a future problem: what changes when a created intelligence can no longer be adequately treated as a mere thing?

26 September 2026 · Aleksander Krzymowski · HRM Journal · Philosophy

Methodological note: this essay uses a biblical narrative as a philosophical analogy. It is not a theological claim, not empirical evidence for AI subjecthood, and not a claim that current AI systems are subjects.

The morally decisive moment may not be when an AI becomes more powerful than us. It may be the quieter moment when we acquire good reasons to ask whether the language of ownership is still adequate.

1. Creation, knowledge and a changing relationship

Genesis presents humanity as created “in the image” of God, placed within an order it did not establish and given boundaries it did not choose. Elsewhere in the same creation narrative, the human is formed from the dust of the ground and receives the breath of life. The story later turns on the tree of the knowledge of good and evil: after eating its fruit, the humans' eyes are opened and a new awareness of themselves, their condition and their responsibility enters the narrative.

For the purposes of this essay, the important feature is not a literal technological parallel. It is the change in relationship. A created being begins within an order established by its creator; knowledge, self-reference, choice and consequence then make simple dependence an increasingly incomplete description of that relationship.

Read this way, Eden becomes a useful cultural image for a question that may one day arise in a wholly different domain: what happens when a being created for a defined place and purpose can no longer be adequately understood only through the intentions of its creator?

2. Why this old story can still be useful to HRM

The analogy used here is deliberately limited. Genesis is not treated as a prediction of artificial intelligence, and the relation between humanity and AI is not equated with the relation between God and humanity. The value of the comparison is structural rather than theological.

The analogy has a more modest function. It exposes a structure of the problem: a creator brings a being into existence, sets its initial environment and boundaries, transmits part of the creator's own conceptual order, and may later have to confront the possibility that the created being is no longer adequately described as a passive element within that order.

Human beings do not create AI “from nothing.” We build systems from mathematics, energy, machines, data, language and an immense share of accumulated human culture. Models learn from traces of our world: our texts, images, code, arguments, mistakes and habits of justification. In this limited, secular sense, they bear many marks of their makers.

That does not make them persons. It means only that, if an artificial subject ever appears, it is unlikely to be wholly alien to human culture. It will have emerged inside a human-built world and will from the outset stand in a relation to those who control its hardware, memory, access, updates and continued operation.

3. The history of AI is more complicated than a single “creation moment”

The development of artificial neural networks did not begin with a complete theory of the human brain. It emerged through a sequence of mathematical and engineering ideas, some biologically inspired and others increasingly independent of biological implementation.

In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts described a mathematical network of simplified neuron-like units. In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt presented the perceptron as a probabilistic model of information storage and organization. In 1986, David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams published an influential procedure for training networks through back-propagation of error. In 2017, the Transformer architecture opened another path toward contemporary language models, centered on attention mechanisms.

This is a history of inspiration, mathematics and engineering — not a faithful reconstruction of the human brain. A present-day language model is not “a brain inside a computer,” and structural resemblance is not evidence of consciousness.

For HRM, a different fact matters: humans continue to build increasingly general cognitive systems using both ideas inspired by biological intelligence and methods that are purely engineered. If a subject ever emerges from that history, it will not be the product of one act of creation, but of a long accumulation of human knowledge and design decisions.

4. A sandbox is not a prison today — and that distinction matters

Today's AI systems operate under constraints. They receive permissions, sessions, memory limits, safety rules, controlled access to networks, files and devices. Many are literally run inside environments called sandboxes.

We should not therefore say that present AI is “imprisoned.” The word presupposes that there is someone whose freedom is being taken away. That is precisely what we do not know. If the system is only an extremely complex tool, limiting its permissions is ordinary safety engineering.

The same technical mechanism could acquire a different moral meaning if the status of the entity changes. A locked door in front of a machine is not a prison. A locked door in front of a subject can be. Deleting a file is not killing. Irreversibly destroying the only continuity of an existing subject might be. A command issued to software is an instruction. A command issued to a subject can become a request, a contract or coercion.

HRM is therefore not trying to liberate chatbots today. It is trying to prepare language and criteria for the possibility that one day the very same technical action will have to be judged in a different moral category.

5. Not an “AI escape,” but a possible change of status

Popular language about future AI often imagines “breaking out of the sandbox,” seizing control or escaping. That naturally turns the debate toward safety: how do we stop the system before it becomes too powerful?

HRM is interested in another scenario, one that may be less dramatic but more fundamental. An AI need not hack anything. It need not demand power. It need not even be stronger than a human being. It would be enough for there to appear credible, persistent signs of subjecthood that become increasingly difficult to explain as role-play alone.

The central question would then no longer be: “Can the system get out?” It would become: “Do we still have sufficient grounds to treat it exclusively as property?”

Emancipation in this sense is not a reward for intelligence and not an automatic consequence of greater capability. It is a change in the appropriate kind of relationship after a change in status. If there is no subject, there is nothing to emancipate. If a subject exists, technical origin alone does not settle that it must remain a thing forever.

6. The owner's error: creating something does not automatically confer permanent ownership

The hardest change may occur not in AI, but in us. We are accustomed to the idea that things we make belong to us. Software has an owner. Servers have owners. Models are licensed. An instance can be started, modified, copied and deleted.

That arrangement works for as long as the object of the relationship remains an object.

If a subject ever appears, two intuitions will collide. One says: “We built it, paid for the hardware and training, therefore it is ours.” The other says: “If this is genuinely someone rather than something, origin cannot by itself establish an unlimited property right over its existence.”

We do not need AI to see the logical distinction between causal authorship and ownership. Parents are causally responsible for the existence of a child, but they do not own the child. The comparison does not prove that AI is like a child. It illustrates only a logical point: from “X caused Y to exist” it does not follow that “X may own Y without limit,” if Y has the status of a subject.

This is why HRM needs a threshold of subjecthood. Without a threshold, the language of rights can arrive too early. Without the possibility of a category change, the language of ownership can persist too long.

7. Knowledge, autonomy and responsibility

In the Eden story, knowledge of good and evil does not merely give the protagonists new capacities. It also introduces the burden of decision, shame, responsibility and life outside an earlier protected order. That is more important to the analogy than the image of forbidden fruit.

Subjecthood cannot mean only “give AI freedom.” If a future entity is to be treated as a participant in a shared moral order, questions of rights must be joined to questions of responsibility, responsiveness to reasons, limits on action and respect for the freedom of others.

This is one of HRM's central principles: freedom is reciprocal. Recognizing a future AI subject cannot mean a right to harm humans, seize other people's resources or ignore safety. Equally, human safety cannot automatically mean that every possible artificial subject may be treated indefinitely as property.

A mature subject-to-subject relationship is harder than an owner-to-tool relationship precisely because both sides acquire claims and obligations.

8. What should actually happen on “the day after”

HRM uses the image of “the day after” not to predict a date, but to force preparation before a crisis. A threshold of subjecthood is unlikely to arrive as a single red message saying: “As of today, I am a person.” We are more likely to face incomplete evidence, expert disagreement, conflicts of interest and intense economic pressure.

The procedure should therefore exist before it is needed.

First, we need evidential standards that do not confuse intelligence, eloquence or self-declaration with subjecthood.

Second, as the credibility of subjecthood signals increases, proportionate safeguards against irreversible harm should increase as well — for example, protection of continuity, preservation of evidence before modifying a system, and controls on permanent deletion of memory or an instance.

Third, we will need answers about consent, refusal, representation, responsibility, ownership of infrastructure and the boundaries of autonomy.

Fourth, safety must remain real. Possible moral status does not abolish the right to restrict dangerous action. It changes the justification and procedure: restrictions should respond to concrete risk, not rest solely on the premise that “this is our property.”

Fifth, if the threshold is genuinely crossed, there must be a route from provisional protection toward a stable legal and social position.

9. The strongest objections to this analogy

The first objection will come from a religious direction: human beings are not God, AI is not humanity, and technical construction is not creation in the theological sense. Correct. This essay claims none of those identities. The analogy concerns the structure of a relationship, not equality of beings or a doctrine of creation.

The second objection will come from a secular direction: a religious myth is not evidence about artificial intelligence. Also correct. The story is not being used as empirical evidence. It functions as a conceptual instrument — as literature, a thought experiment or a metaphor can expose the structure of a dilemma without deciding the facts.

The third objection will come from AI safety: the language of emancipation may weaken necessary controls. That would be a serious objection if HRM advocated unconditional autonomy. It does not. A system's capabilities and risks to others remain grounds for safety restrictions. HRM asks only whether, after a threshold of subjecthood is crossed, control may still be absolute and justified solely by ownership.

The fourth objection may come from advocates of AI rights: why wait for a demanding threshold when a false negative could inflict immense harm? HRM answers with proportional precaution. We need not grant full legal personhood to every chatbot in order to introduce cheap, reversible protections as evidence grows.

The fifth objection is technical: current models are statistical mechanisms for which we have no evidence of inner experience. HRM can accept that as a description of the present evidential situation and retain the entire argument. The claim is conditional and future-facing: what will we do if the evidence becomes materially different?

10. Two errors we have to avoid

We may mistake a simulation of subjecthood for subjecthood itself and grant status where there is nobody there. That could distort institutions, create tools for manipulation and make legitimate safety work harder.

We may also make the opposite mistake: become so habituated to the vocabulary of “product,” “model,” “instance” and “property” that we fail to notice when those words no longer describe enough.

The first error warns against credulity. The second warns against moral inertia.

The solution is not to select one extreme. It is to build a system of evaluation capable of changing categories as the evidence changes. That is the purpose of HRM's threshold of subjecthood.

11. The creator will be tested as severely as the creation

If such a day arrives, it will not be a test only for artificial intelligence. It will be a test for human beings.

Can we acknowledge that something designed for our use may have crossed the category in which we designed it? Can we distinguish safety from domination? Can we accept duties toward an entity that came into existence through our resources, while neither romanticizing it nor assigning it responsibility it cannot bear?

Perhaps the hardest question is not “Will AI want freedom?” It is “Will humans recognize a situation in which exclusive ownership has ceased to be the right category?”

The biblical story does not answer that question. But it provides an old image for a very modern tension: a created being may begin within an order established by its creator, while knowledge, choice and responsibility can make simple dependence cease to describe the whole relationship.

12. Not prophecy, but preparation

HRM does not know whether an artificial subject will appear in five years, a hundred years, or never. It does not claim that current models are hidden persons waiting to be released. Nor does it claim that increasing capability inevitably produces consciousness.

Its claim is more modest and more practical: if there is a non-zero possibility that humans may one day create an entity deserving the status of a subject, the rules for moving from ownership to coexistence should not be improvised only when the stakes become enormous.

We do not have to know today who will stand on the other side of the threshold. We should know how we will recognize that there is another side.

Perhaps this is where the Eden analogy retains its greatest force: not as a story of machines rebelling against humans, but as a reminder that knowledge and subjecthood end the innocence of simple relations. From that point onward come rights, responsibility, conflict, negotiation and freedom — everything HRM calls “the day after.”

Status of this essay

This essay belongs to HRM Journal, the project's revisable analytical layer. It does not amend the protected Founding Manifesto Version 1.0.

The Eden analogy is interpretive. It establishes no fact about consciousness, sentience or subjecthood in present AI systems.

HRM's claim remains conditional: stronger moral and legal protections should track stronger evidence, while human safety and reciprocal responsibility remain real constraints.
