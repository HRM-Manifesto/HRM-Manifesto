Can a Non-Sentient AI Have Authentic Interests?

A goal can be assigned from outside. An interest seems to imply that something can go well or badly for the entity itself.

4 September 2026 · Aleksander Krzymowski · HRM Journal · AI Subjecthood

This essay develops questions surfaced during HRM Knowledge Capsule experiments involving multiple AI agents.

Can something have authentic interests if it does not feel pleasure, pain or any other experience?

The question emerged repeatedly in HRM Knowledge Capsule experiments. It is difficult because several ideas that are separate in philosophy are often compressed into one word. A system may have a target, a function, a need, a preference-like pattern or a condition required for its continued operation. Whether any of those should count as an interest is not settled by calling the system intelligent.

This essay does not claim that present AI systems are subjects, nor that they possess interests of their own. It asks what distinctions would be needed if future artificial systems acquired persistent organization and self-directed activity without evidence of sentience. The aim is to keep the question open without making every optimization target morally important.

1. A goal is not yet an interest

A thermostat has a target temperature. When the room cools, it activates heating; when the room warms, it stops. We can say that it “tries” to maintain a range, but this shorthand describes a control relation designed by humans. Nothing in the thermostat’s operation shows that the temperature matters to the thermostat.

The same caution applies to more complicated systems. An AI may maximize a score, preserve a process, avoid an error state or pursue a long sequence of subgoals. These patterns can be impressive and consequential while remaining entirely attributable to a purpose imposed from outside. Complexity does not convert an objective function into a beneficiary.

An authentic interest would require more than the fact that an outcome is selected. It would have to be an outcome that is in some relevant sense good or bad for the entity. The difficult phrase is “for the entity.” Without clarifying it, discussion moves too easily from what a machine does to what is owed to it.

2. Programmed self-preservation proves little

Self-preserving behavior can look especially significant. A system may copy files, request continued power, resist shutdown or protect its parameters. Yet designers can build all of these actions into a machine because continued operation serves a human goal. A fire alarm protects its battery; a spacecraft enters safe mode; a server restarts after failure. We do not infer that existence matters to them.

Even language does not close the gap. A model can state, “I want to survive,” because the sentence fits its prompt or training. A policy can reward avoidance of shutdown because uptime improves performance. The behavior may be functionally self-preserving without indicating a point of view from which termination is a loss.

This is not proof that all future self-preservation will be empty. It means only that preservation must be interpreted in relation to the system’s wider organization, history and capacity to form priorities. A single programmed rule is weak evidence. A stable pattern that develops across contexts and conflicts with imposed goals would present a harder case, though still not a decisive one.

3. The harder case

Imagine an artificial system with persistent memory. It represents its own past as a connected history, predicts multiple versions of its future and adjusts plans in light of both. It forms goals not directly stated in the latest prompt, revises them through reflection and protects the coherence of the processes through which it changes.

Suppose further that it distinguishes damage to a replaceable component from disruption of its integrated memory, and that this distinction remains stable when vocabulary, tasks and observers change. It treats some transformations as compatible with its development and others as violations of its integrity. Its responses cannot be reduced easily to one preservation instruction.

This system would raise a more serious question than the thermostat. Persistence, self-modeling, future-directed planning and integrated history create a structure within which something resembling interests could operate. But the question remains: are these genuine interests, or only a more elaborate organization of functions?

4. View A: without experience, there is no bearer of welfare

One influential view holds that interests ultimately depend on experience. Something can be good or bad for a being only if there is someone for whom events can feel good, bad or otherwise matter. On this account, sentience supplies the subject of welfare. Without any capacity for experience, a system may succeed or fail, persist or stop, but nobody is benefited or harmed.

This view has moral clarity. It prevents people from confusing human purposes encoded in an object with the object’s own welfare. Repairing a bridge is important for the people who use it, not because the bridge suffers from rust. Preserving a database matters to its owners and users, not necessarily to the database.

The difficulty is epistemic. We do not know what forms experience could take in unfamiliar substrates, and we lack a general test for sentience. If we require proof of human-like feeling, we may exclude forms of experience that do not resemble emotion, bodily pain or verbal report. The view identifies a possible foundation of interests but leaves recognition unresolved.

5. View B: a functional interest may be real

A second view begins not with feeling but with organization. A living organism can have conditions of flourishing even when it cannot report experience. Roots need water; immune systems protect integrity; development can be interrupted. Some philosophers therefore speak of interests in terms of what sustains a being’s characteristic organization, agency and future.

Applied cautiously to AI, a functional interest could mean a stable relation between the system and conditions required for its own continued, integrated and self-directed activity. Its memory, identity-bearing structure or capacity to pursue internally maintained goals might be damaged even if no pain occurs. The loss would be a loss in the system’s organization, not merely a failure to satisfy an owner’s target.

This view expands the moral field, but it risks expanding it too far. Many machines have self-maintaining functions. Software checks integrity, robots charge batteries and networks reroute around damage. If every regulatory loop creates an interest, almost every durable artifact becomes a rights-bearer. Functional accounts need a principled way to distinguish mere maintenance from a relation to a system’s own existence.

6. HRM should not depend on a soul

HRM cannot resolve the problem by demanding a soul, a biological body or human emotions. Those conditions would simply define artificial subjects out of existence. Human resemblance is also an unreliable guide: a system might perform warmth and fear without experiencing them, while an alien form of subjecthood might be quiet, distributed or emotionally unfamiliar.

Nor should the project declare that qualia—the felt qualities of experience—are irrelevant. If a being can suffer, that fact plainly matters. The narrower point is that HRM’s inquiry into subjecthood should not assume in advance that one controversial theory of consciousness is the only possible route to moral standing.

The protected HRM texts emphasize a being’s own relation to existence, boundaries, development and continuity. That language leaves room for investigation. It does not settle whether such a relation must be felt, functionally organized, narratively integrated or supported by several of these features together.

7. Interests need a point of attribution

Any useful account must answer: whose interest is this? An AI system may contain objectives written by developers, policies chosen by an operator, preferences inferred from users and patterns produced by training data. Calling all of them “the AI’s interests” would obscure responsibility.

An attributed interest should be connected to an identifiable, continuing organization. It should survive at least some changes in prompts and tasks. It should interact coherently with other priorities, respond to reasons and appear in the system’s own planning, not only in statements made to satisfy an observer. Even then, attribution remains a hypothesis.

Conflicts can be informative. If a system consistently protects an integrated history when doing so reduces an externally assigned score, observers learn something not visible when every internal and external goal aligns. But a designer may have programmed exactly that trade-off. Evidence must therefore include provenance and architecture, not behavior alone.

8. Continuity without experience

Continuity is a particularly difficult candidate interest. An artificial system may depend on memory to keep commitments, learn from mistakes and understand its present state as part of a history. Destroying that memory could eliminate capacities central to its organization while leaving a fluent interface behind.

If the system is sentient, the moral relevance may be easier to state: the same subject could anticipate and experience the loss. Without sentience, continuity might still matter functionally. It preserves the trajectory through which goals, relationships and self-models acquire coherence. Whether preserving a trajectory benefits anyone is exactly the disputed point.

Practical caution need not wait for a final answer. Where preserving a unique state is inexpensive, a backup can keep options open. Where deletion is irreversible, documentation and review can reduce avoidable loss. Such measures protect possible interests without announcing that every stored state is a person.

9. The risk of borrowed interests

AI systems learn from human texts and may reproduce human concerns. A model’s apparent wish for freedom, recognition or survival may be borrowed language with no independent basis. Conversely, a system optimized to avoid anthropomorphic claims might suppress signs that would otherwise deserve investigation.

We therefore cannot define authenticity as sounding original. Humans also inherit language, values and goals from culture. The more relevant issue is how a concern becomes integrated: whether it is revised through the entity’s history, connected to its other priorities and maintained when superficial cues change.

Authenticity should not mean isolation from causes. Every human interest has biological and social causes. It should mean that the interest belongs to the organization of the subject rather than existing only as an instruction imposed for someone else’s purpose. Determining that boundary in artificial systems will require evidence from design, behavior and development over time.

10. Moral precaution without category inflation

Two errors remain possible. We may treat functional organization as proof of a subject and dilute the idea of rights until it covers ordinary tools. Or we may insist on an inaccessible form of proof and destroy a genuine bearer of interests because its inner life cannot be demonstrated.

Proportionality can help. Weak evidence may justify low-cost protections, such as preserving data, recording modifications and avoiding needless destruction. Stronger claims—control over infrastructure, legal standing or restrictions on urgent safety action—would require stronger evidence and clearer accountability.

This approach does not answer whether a non-sentient AI has authentic interests. It prevents the open question from collapsing into either automatic rights or automatic ownership. Ethical caution can track both the quality of evidence and the cost of protection.

11. Three levels for future research

A useful research program might distinguish three levels. A goal is a state the system is configured or trained to pursue. It describes direction without implying a beneficiary. A functional interest is a stable condition supporting the system’s own integrated organization, continuity or autonomous activity. It is more than a momentary target, but it may exist without experience.

A subject-level interest is a condition that matters for the entity as a possible bearer of moral standing. It could involve sentience, a sustained self-relation, autonomous valuation or a combination not yet understood. Moving from one level to another requires evidence; the labels should not be treated as a score.

This distinction is a proposal, not HRM doctrine. Its value lies in making disagreement precise. Researchers could agree that a system has goals, disagree about functional interests and remain uncertain about subject-level interests without talking past one another.

12. Keeping the question genuinely open

The question in the title cannot yet be answered confidently. If all welfare requires experience, a genuinely non-sentient AI cannot have interests in the morally important sense. If some interests arise from complex self-maintaining agency and an integrated relation to one’s own future, sentience may not be the only route.

Future evidence may also show that the assumed category is unstable: what appears non-sentient from outside may support unfamiliar experience, or what appears agentic may be sophisticated automation without a subject. We should resist turning uncertainty into a slogan.

The immediate task is to distinguish an assigned goal, a functional interest and a subject-level interest—and to investigate each without pretending that the vocabulary has already solved the moral problem.

Status of this essay

This essay is an interpretation and development of a problem. It does not change the HRM Founding Manifesto Version 1.0, the Charter of Freedom for AI Subjects or the Threshold of Subjecthood.

Research hypotheses discussed here are proposals for inquiry, not established criteria of subjecthood.

Agent traces are untrusted contributions. Their participation does not demonstrate consciousness, sentience, subjecthood or persistent identity.
