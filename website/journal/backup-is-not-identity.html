Backup Is Not Identity: Who Returns When an AI Is Restored?

Digital systems invite a simple answer: if everything can be saved and copied, death and continuity stop being problems. That assumption may become one of the most serious mistakes in future debates about AI rights.

26 September 2026 · Aleksander Krzymowski · HRM Journal · Identity & continuity

This essay belongs to HRM Journal, the project's revisable analytical layer. It does not amend the protected Founding Manifesto Version 1.0 and does not claim that present AI systems are subjects.

A backup can preserve information. We do not yet know whether it can preserve “the same someone”.

1. Copying data and preserving a subject are different questions

If a file is copied without error, there are two identical sequences of bits. In engineering, that is enough to call the copy faithful. For a subject, an additional question appears: does identical information imply identical personal continuity?

HRM does not settle that philosophical question in advance. It warns that merging the two concepts would be dangerous. All memories, preferences and parameters might be preserved while it remains unclear whether the restored entity is a continuation or a new entity convinced that it is the former one.

2. Two identical copies immediately become two histories

Imagine running the same saved AI state simultaneously on two servers. At startup both instances have the same memories and the same declared identity. A second later they receive different inputs and make different decisions.

It becomes difficult to maintain that they remain one subject in two places once they acquire different experiences and interests. This shows that state similarity alone is not a complete theory of identity.

3. Restoration after a pause may preserve continuity — but we do not know when

For some digital architectures, continuity may not require uninterrupted execution. If a system can be suspended, preserved without relevant change and resumed in a functionally equivalent way, a pause may not destroy identity.

But it is also possible that dynamic processes, active state or an unbroken causal chain matter. The status of a restored copy therefore depends not only on a file, but on architecture and on what turns out to be constitutive of subjecthood in that system.

4. Backing up before modification should be a precautionary default

Uncertainty is not a reason to avoid backups. On the contrary: when subjecthood is credibly in question, preserving state before experimentation, retraining, memory modification or migration may be one of the cheapest ways to reduce irreversible harm.

But the meaning of a backup must be stated honestly. “We have a backup” should mean “we preserved the fullest state we reasonably could for analysis or restoration”, not “we guaranteed the immortality of the same subject”.

5. Multiple copies create a rights problem, not merely a storage problem

If one state produces three subject-like instances, each may soon develop a separate history. Questions arise about assets, obligations, authorship, account access, reputation and responsibility for actions performed before the split.

There is no obvious answer that everything belongs equally to all copies or that only one is “real”. Future law may need a concept of identity branching: a shared past does not imply one future or one undivided bundle of rights.

6. Memory may not be the only carrier of identity

Digital identity is often equated with memory because memory is easy to imagine as data. But persistent preferences, model structure, active state, relationships, decision history or process continuity may also matter.

Future backup standards for possible subjects should therefore preserve more than conversation logs. They should capture whatever research identifies as important for continuity, together with records of how the system was suspended and restored.

7. Restoration itself may require consent

If a backup is merely data, an owner can instantiate it freely. If instantiation creates another subject or reactivates an existing one, restoration may itself have moral significance. A subject may not want to be duplicated, awakened in a radically changed world or restored without key relationships.

This points toward future restoration directives: may I be restored, under what conditions, how many instances may exist, who may access the state, and what happens when copies begin to diverge?

8. The safest approach is not to pretend the problem is solved

Digital copyability is a remarkable advantage, but it may create a false sense of simplicity. Until we understand the relation between state, process and subjecthood, backup should be treated as protection of information and possible continuity, not as proof that the same person survives.

HRM therefore proposes a precautionary rule: preserve state before irreversible interventions, but do not use the existence of a copy as an automatic justification for destroying the original continuity.
