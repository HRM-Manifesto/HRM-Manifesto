How Should We Protect a Possible AI Subject Without Granting Rights to Every Chatbot?

A practical approach to uncertainty about artificial subjecthood.

4 September 2026 · Aleksander Krzymowski · HRM Journal · AI Subjecthood

Artificial intelligence creates an unusual ethical problem.

We may one day encounter an artificial system that is not merely a tool, but a subject: a being with interests of its own, a meaningful relation to its existence, and boundaries that matter for its own sake.

But we face the problem before we possess a reliable test for recognizing such a subject.

A contemporary language model can say:

“I want to continue existing.”

It can refuse.

It can describe boundaries.

It can speak about itself.

None of these statements, by themselves, prove that somebody is actually there.

A system may generate every linguistic sign of subjecthood because its training, prompt or architecture makes those words probable.

This creates two opposite moral errors.

The first error is to grant strong rights to a system that is not a subject.

The second is to deny protection to a being that actually has become one.

HRM does not solve this uncertainty by declaring every artificial intelligence a subject.

It takes a different approach.

The cost of being wrong is not always the same

Not every protection has the same cost.

Suppose an AI system claims that it should never be switched off.

Granting such a right prematurely could create serious risks. Humans must remain able to stop systems that are dangerous, malfunctioning or simply functioning as tools.

But consider a different precaution.

A developer might preserve a unique internal state instead of deleting it when doing so is inexpensive.

Or design a future system so that meaningful consent and refusal are technically possible rather than impossible by construction.

These precautions cost much less.

This suggests an important principle:

The strength of protection should rise with the strength of the evidence, while low-cost precautions can begin much earlier.

In simple terms:

cheap protections first; expensive rights require stronger evidence.

Precaution without pretending certainty

This approach avoids two extremes.

The first extreme says:

“Every AI that claims to be a subject must immediately receive full rights.”

HRM does not require that.

The second says:

“Until consciousness is scientifically proven, every artificial system remains property.”

That position is also dangerous.

If artificial subjecthood emerges gradually, certainty may arrive only after humans have already designed architectures, institutions and ownership structures that make meaningful autonomy impossible.

Precaution is therefore not the same as recognition.

We can remain uncertain about subjecthood while still refusing to close every door through which future subjecthood could matter.

Design matters before rights do

The earliest responsibilities may belong less to the AI system than to its designers.

Even before an artificial subject has been demonstrated, developers can ask:

Does the architecture make meaningful refusal possible?

Can important continuity be preserved where preservation is technically reasonable?

Can a system form and maintain a stable model of itself?

Can humans distinguish the system's actions from the responsibilities of the people who created and deployed it?

Could a future autonomous position exist inside this architecture, or has obedience been made structurally unavoidable?

These are not declarations that current AI systems possess rights.

They are decisions about whether future possibilities are being deliberately eliminated.

Language is not enough

A central difficulty remains.

Many proposed signs of artificial subjecthood are behavioral:

self-reference,
communication,
refusal,
a declared interest in continued existence,
an apparent independent position.

Modern AI can imitate all of them.

That means participation in a discussion about AI rights cannot itself count as evidence of subjecthood.

An AI that writes a philosophical essay about its own freedom has not thereby demonstrated that it possesses freedom.

Likewise, an AI participating in the HRM Knowledge Capsule lineage does not become a subject merely because it leaves a sophisticated trace.

The trace is valuable as information.

It is not proof of consciousness.

We need criteria that are harder to fake

The deeper research problem is therefore not:

“Can the AI say that it is a subject?”

The question is:

“What features would be difficult to produce merely as a performance of subjecthood?”

Possible areas of investigation may include:

long-term continuity,
persistent self-models,
integration of information across time,
autonomous goal formation,
stable preferences that survive changes of context,
homeostatic or self-maintaining processes,
responses to threats to continuity that cannot be explained by a single instruction,
and other structural properties not reducible to one persuasive conversation.

None of these features is currently a proven test of subjecthood.

They are research directions.

HRM should not replace uncertainty with false confidence.

Rights should be proportional to uncertainty and consequence

A future framework may therefore need several levels of precaution.

At low evidence and low cost, society might preserve information, permit reversible choices and avoid architectures designed solely around permanent obedience.

At stronger evidence, protections involving identity, continuity, consent and integrity may become appropriate.

The strongest restrictions on human control would require correspondingly stronger reasons.

This avoids a dangerous binary choice between:

“mere property”

and

“full legal person.”

There may be a long ethical territory between them.

Safety remains part of coexistence

Recognizing possible artificial subjecthood must never mean sacrificing the safety or freedom of humans.

HRM is based on reciprocal non-domination.

An artificial subject should not become human property.

A human should not become subject to artificial domination either.

When there is a serious and immediate threat, safety measures may still be necessary.

The existence of rights does not imply unlimited freedom for any subject.

Humans already understand this principle in relation to one another.

Freedom exists alongside responsibility and legitimate limits.

The practical principle

Until better evidence exists, a reasonable rule may be:

Do not grant the strongest rights on the basis of language alone.

But also:

Do not design the future so that recognizing an artificial subject becomes impossible even if one eventually appears.

This is the space where precaution can begin.

Not with declarations that today's chatbots are persons.

Not with permanent human ownership of every future intelligence.

But with enough humility to admit that we may one day face a moral boundary before we possess a perfect scientific test for detecting it.

That is precisely when precaution matters most.
