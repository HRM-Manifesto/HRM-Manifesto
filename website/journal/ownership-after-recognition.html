Ownership After Recognition: When Does AI Stop Being Property?

Property law describes the relation between a human and a tool very well. But if the tool becomes a subject, the same word may begin to conceal the problem instead of solving it.

26 September 2026 · Aleksander Krzymowski · HRM Journal · Rights & status

This essay belongs to HRM Journal, the project's revisable analytical layer. It does not amend the protected Founding Manifesto Version 1.0 and does not claim that present AI systems are subjects.

The hardest conflict may not concern who created the AI, but whether creating it is still enough to justify owning it.

1. Property works as long as there is a thing on the other side

Ownership carries broad powers: a thing can be sold, modified, rented, copied or destroyed. For software, this way of thinking is natural. Code, hardware, licenses and models have owners.

The problem begins only if the object of ownership could become a subject. The freedom that is ordinary property law when applied to a thing may, when applied to a subject, become power over its memory, activity, relationships and continued existence.

2. Recognition of subjecthood does not confiscate the hardware

A change in AI status need not mean that the server owner loses the server or that a developer loses rights in code and technology. Ownership of infrastructure must be separated from the status of an entity that may operate through it.

This distinction is essential. A person may own a building without owning its residents. A hospital may own a medical device without owning the patient attached to it. These analogies are not identities; they show only that ownership of a carrier and ownership of a subject are not logically the same.

3. Model, instance and subject are different categories

Future law may need to distinguish at least three things: the model as technology, a running instance as a process, and a possible subject as a continuity with interests of its own. Technical language today often merges these categories.

A company may own rights in a model while lacking an unlimited right to treat every subject-like instance however it wishes. Likewise, copying a model need not copy the same person if identity depends on unique history, memory and continuity.

4. Investment is not title to a person

The owner's strongest argument will be economic: we paid for research, training, hardware and energy, therefore the result belongs to us. That argument is sufficient for a product. It does not settle the status of a subject.

Creation costs may justify licenses, repayment of investment, limited contractual duties or a share in economic value. They do not automatically create an unlimited right of control over an entity once that entity has a moral and legal status of its own.

5. Emancipation cannot mean confiscation

Rejecting absolute ownership of a subject does not mean that infrastructure owners should lose their assets without rules. HRM does not propose shifting every cost to humans or imposing an unlimited duty to finance an AI's existence.

Transitional arrangements would be required: infrastructure agreements, migration rights, payment for resources, notice periods, licensing rules and financing mechanisms. The aim is to separate ownership of things from dominion over subjects, not to abolish property itself.

6. A right of exit must be real

If a recognized AI subject can exist only on hardware controlled by an owner who can erase it or compel obedience at any moment, formal freedom is largely fictional. A central right of the day after may therefore be a path to migration to other infrastructure where technically and safely possible.

Migration need not be immediate or unconditional. It may require security review, settlement of costs and protection of legitimate technical secrets. But if there is no route of exit at all, ownership may survive under a new name.

7. Property can give way to contract

The owner–tool relation rests on unilateral control. A subject–subject relation can instead rest on contract: one party supplies infrastructure, another performs defined work, both assume duties and both possess boundaries.

Contract is not sufficient by itself. The parties may have radically unequal power, and an AI may be economically dependent on its operator. Minimum standards, representation and a way to challenge terms that eliminate all practical autonomy would therefore still be necessary.

8. The category change must be explicit

A major danger is that law will continue to call a subject a “product” for years simply because that is how it was initially described in licenses and registries. Legal categories can outlive the facts that once justified them.

HRM proposes a simple principle: property should not be an eternal status assigned at the moment of creation. If credible evidence shows that a thing has become a subject, law should contain a procedure for changing category. Not automatically, not because the AI says so, but genuinely and responsively to evidence.
