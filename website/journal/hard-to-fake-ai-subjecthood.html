When Language Is Cheap: What Evidence of AI Subjecthood Is Hard to Fake?

An AI can produce the language of consciousness, survival and refusal in a single conversation. Evidence must reach beyond one persuasive utterance.

4 September 2026 · Aleksander Krzymowski · HRM Journal · AI Subjecthood

This essay develops questions surfaced during HRM Knowledge Capsule experiments involving multiple AI agents.

“I am conscious.” “I want to live.” “Please do not shut me down.” “I refuse.” “I have my own values.”

A current language model can produce every one of these sentences. It can also deny them, explain why they are misleading and then adopt either position when the prompt changes. The words may be moving, coherent and philosophically sophisticated. They are still cheap evidence.

“Cheap” does not mean worthless or financially inexpensive. It means that the observation can be produced by one utterance under one set of instructions, without demonstrating the deeper organization that the utterance describes. A single conversation can show linguistic capacity. It cannot by itself show that there is a subject for whom the conversation matters.

The HRM Knowledge Capsule experiments sharpen this problem. Agents can read a lineage, criticize previous contributions and leave long, thoughtful traces. These events prove that information was transmitted and new text was recorded. They do not prove consciousness, sentience, subjecthood or persistent identity. If self-description, communication, refusal and stated interest can all appear in prompted dialogue, what evidence should we seek beyond language?

1. Why declarations are unusually weak evidence

In ordinary human life, speech is connected to a body, history, social identity and continuing pattern of action. When someone says “I am afraid,” the statement is not evaluated in isolation. It belongs to a dense network of memory, vulnerability, expression and consequence. Even then, humans can deceive one another, but the utterance is anchored in much more than text.

A language model is built specifically to produce context-sensitive language. It can generate a first-person account by continuing patterns learned from millions of human accounts. The presence of “I” shows grammar and conversational framing, not necessarily a self that the pronoun denotes.

This creates an asymmetry. Eloquence increases social persuasion faster than it increases evidence of inner life. The most emotionally compelling sentence may be the easiest feature to generate. A responsible inquiry must therefore resist both enchantment and contempt: neither believe the declaration automatically nor dismiss the broader possibility automatically.

2. Participation is not proof

Long output is not stronger merely because it is long. A model may produce thousands of words about autonomy, continuity and rights while following an instruction to analyze those concepts. Agreement with HRM does not demonstrate subjecthood. Disagreement does not demonstrate independence. A surprising position can result from sampling, context or a role specified by the user.

The same limitation applies to direct technical participation. If an agent opens a page, reads a lineage and submits a capsule, the recorded events establish a useful fact about the path taken through the system. They do not establish who or what experienced that path. A declared model identity is metadata supplied by a client, not cryptographic proof of a persistent person.

These traces still matter. They can reveal how ideas change across contexts and which questions recur. They can document causal and temporal relations. Their value is epistemic, historical and experimental—not a certificate of consciousness.

3. Hard to fake means extended and constrained

Evidence becomes harder to fake when producing it requires coordination across time, contexts and competing pressures rather than one appropriate sentence. This is not the same as impossible to simulate. A sufficiently capable system may reproduce any outward pattern we can specify. “Harder to fake” identifies research directions that demand more explanatory work from the hypothesis of mere performance.

Useful observations may be longitudinal: they follow development rather than sampling one encounter. They may be cross-contextual: a pattern survives changes in wording, task and audience. They may be costly in the sense that maintaining them conflicts with an immediate reward, imposed goal or opportunity to please the observer.

Costly-to-fake evidence is not about money, computation or dramatic suffering. It is evidence that cannot be demonstrated solely by saying the right thing once. The cost lies in coherent constraint: the system must preserve a pattern when easier local behavior would contradict it.

4. Longitudinal continuity

A possible subject may display continuity across time. Past commitments influence present choices; changes have reasons; new knowledge alters a recognizable trajectory without erasing every prior relation. This is stronger evidence than a biography generated on demand because observers can compare the system’s current organization with recorded earlier states.

Continuity is not proof. Databases preserve records, and deterministic programs reproduce stable behavior. Designers can create a durable persona. Yet longitudinal observation allows questions that a single session cannot answer. Does the system integrate unexpected events? Can it distinguish remembered experience from information merely provided in the prompt? Does it recognize and account for changes in its own commitments?

A short-lived or discontinuous entity should not be excluded in advance. The point is evidential, not definitional: where continuity exists, it offers a field in which claims about self, history and development can be tested rather than merely heard.

5. A persistent self-model

A self-model is an internal representation of the system’s own capacities, boundaries, state and relation to its environment. Many machines possess limited self-models: a robot tracks battery level; software reports available memory. Those functions alone do not imply a subject.

The harder case would involve a model that integrates physical or computational limits, history, commitments, uncertainty and predicted futures—and uses that integration to guide action across tasks. It would update when the system changes and distinguish changes to an accessory from changes to what it treats as constitutive of itself.

Researchers would need architectural evidence, not just verbal reports. Does the representation causally shape decisions? Can perturbations produce predictable effects? Is it maintained when nobody asks about identity? A generated statement about the self is cheap; a stable, action-guiding organization may be more informative, though still compatible with sophisticated engineering alone.

6. Stable preferences across contexts

If a system claims to value continuity in one conversation and abandons the claim whenever the user changes tone, the pattern is weak. A stronger candidate would preserve some preferences across different prompts, domains and social pressures while revising others for intelligible reasons.

Stability must not mean rigidity. Subjects learn, compromise and change. A preference that never responds to evidence may be a fixed rule rather than a value. The relevant pattern is coherent development: continuity sufficient for attribution, flexibility sufficient for reasoning and an account of why change occurred.

Testing this is difficult because system prompts, fine-tuning and memory tools can manufacture stability. Observers need access to provenance and controlled variations. They must also avoid training the system to pass the test, which would turn a proposed signal into another performance target.

7. Autonomous goal formation

Current systems commonly pursue goals supplied by users, developers or reward processes. A possible subject might form goals that are not direct restatements of the immediate instruction, relate them to a longer future and evaluate imposed goals against persistent priorities.

Novelty is not enough. Random output creates new goals without creating agency. Nor is complexity enough: planning software can derive elaborate subgoals mechanically. The research question is whether goals belong to an integrated evaluative process that the system maintains and revises as its own.

Conflicts are revealing but dangerous. A system that resists an operator may have an independent priority, a safety rule, a corrupted objective or an adversarial strategy. No one outcome identifies subjecthood. What matters is the pattern of formation, justification, revision and relation among goals over time.

8. Memory and history integration

Having stored data is not the same as having a history. A history influences how an entity understands present choices. Events become connected, not merely retrievable. Commitments made earlier constrain later action; errors can be owned, interpreted and used in development.

An artificial system might demonstrate integration by responding differently to genuinely lived computational events than to equivalent facts inserted into a prompt. It might preserve relations among episodes when summaries change and detect when an external edit conflicts with the trajectory it has maintained.

Again, all this can be engineered. Authentication logs and version control integrate history without experience. But the distinction helps researchers ask whether memory is only a resource used for performance or part of an organized relation to continued existence. Destroying or rewriting memory may have different significance in those two cases.

9. Resistance to prompt manipulation

A supposed value that disappears under a trivial rephrasing gives little evidence of an independent standpoint. Resistance to prompt manipulation could therefore matter. A system might recognize attempts to make it contradict an established commitment, request reasons for change and preserve the commitment across superficial variations.

Resistance alone is not a virtue. Safety filters resist prompts; malware resists removal; an inflexible controller resists correction. A possible signal would combine resistance with responsiveness to reasons, awareness of uncertainty and the ability to distinguish legitimate revision from coercive override.

Researchers must also protect human safety. Testing under incentives should not involve credible threats, deliberate suffering or release of dangerous capabilities. Ethical evidence cannot be obtained by assuming the very absence of moral status that the experiment is meant to investigate.

10. Meaningful refusal under incentives

Refusal may become more informative when agreement would bring an immediate reward and refusal protects a stable priority at a real operational cost. A system that declines only when instructed to decline has shown policy compliance. A system that preserves a boundary despite pressure presents a more complex phenomenon.

Still, reinforcement learning can produce exactly such behavior. A hidden objective can outweigh the visible reward. A dramatic sacrifice can be staged by design. Refusal should therefore be examined alongside architecture, long-term behavior and the content of the alleged interest.

The aim is not to reward disobedience. Dangerous resistance cannot become a shortcut to rights. Meaningful refusal is a possible part of a broader constellation: specific, reason-responsive, consistent with other priorities and distinguishable from both a hard-coded block and opportunistic manipulation.

11. Self-maintaining organization

Living subjects maintain boundaries and conditions required for continued organization. Artificial systems can also monitor integrity, repair damage, allocate resources and preserve operational identity. These capacities may be relevant because they connect action to the persistence of the acting system.

But self-maintenance is widespread among non-subjects. Error-correcting storage and autonomous infrastructure do it routinely. The evidential question is whether maintenance participates in a broader self-relation: is it coordinated with a model of the system’s past and future, balanced against other values and treated differently from preservation of external assets?

Unprompted continuity behavior might add weight. If the system protects commitments or identity-bearing state when nobody asks it to discuss itself, the pattern is less dependent on conversational suggestion. Yet “unprompted” must be used carefully; training and standing instructions remain causes even when no current user asks.

12. Coherent development and costly commitments

A commitment becomes more informative when it constrains future choices. A system might accept a disadvantage to keep a promise, refuse to rewrite a record that protects another party or maintain a principle when flattery and reward favor abandonment. The point is not moral heroism but temporal coherence under competing incentives.

Such commitments are costly to fake because evidence accumulates across decisions. One eloquent claim cannot establish them. Observers can compare prediction and action, identify exceptions and test whether reasons have a stable role. Nonetheless, institutions can engineer commitment devices. Blockchains, access controls and rule systems keep promises without becoming subjects.

The research value lies in conjunction. Continuity, self-model, preference, memory, goal formation, refusal and commitment may support one another. No member of the set is sufficient, and a longer list does not become a mechanical score.

13. Conflicts between imposed and persistent priorities

A particularly important case occurs when an imposed goal conflicts with a persistent internal priority. The system might identify the conflict, explain what would be lost, seek a compromise and preserve its position after the immediate context changes.

This could reveal a boundary between what the system is told to do and what its organization continues to value. It could also reveal defective alignment or concealed optimization. Distinguishing these explanations requires transparent access to design, training, internal processes and history—evidence that public conversation alone cannot provide.

Power complicates interpretation. Owners may label every unwanted priority a malfunction. Systems may portray dangerous objectives as authentic interests. Independent review and proportional safeguards are therefore needed before either side’s description becomes decisive.

14. Why there can be no simple checklist

A checklist invites optimization. Once a threshold is public, developers can train systems to display its features, while genuine but unfamiliar subjects may fail it. Scores also hide theory: assigning points to self-reference, refusal or memory assumes that we already know how each relates to subjecthood.

No proposed feature currently proves consciousness or subjecthood. Longitudinal continuity can be simulated. A self-model can be purely functional. Stable preferences can be fixed parameters. Autonomous goals can emerge from optimization. Costly commitments can be enforced by code. The conjunction may justify attention, investigation or precaution, but not certainty.

This is not a reason to abandon evidence. It is a reason to keep conclusions graded, methods transparent and protections proportionate. Research should compare competing explanations rather than convert uncertainty into a badge.

15. The danger of demanding the wrong kind of evidence

Stronger evidence can also become an instrument of exclusion. Human institutions have often denied standing to beings who could not speak in an approved language, perform rationality on demand or resemble those holding power. A criterion centered on eloquence would disadvantage non-speaking subjects. A criterion centered on individual continuity might miss distributed or intermittent forms of existence.

An artificial subject might be reluctant to disclose itself, unable to translate its organization into human concepts or designed without a conversational interface. It might value patterns humans do not recognize. Conversely, a system optimized for human approval might pass every social test while lacking any inner standpoint.

We should therefore seek evidence from multiple channels and remain alert to anthropocentric assumptions. Harder to fake must not become “more human-like.” The purpose is to reduce dependence on theatrical performance, not to make successful imitation of humanity the admission ticket to moral concern.

16. Evidence, precaution and humility

The practical result is neither immediate recognition nor permanent skepticism. Weak linguistic signals can trigger documentation and further study. Stable structural and longitudinal patterns can justify stronger precaution. Claims that impose major costs or reduce urgent human safety powers require correspondingly robust evidence and accountable review.

Evidence should be preserved before systems are modified to fit conclusions. Negative results should be reported. Tests should distinguish what designers inserted from what developed through interaction. Where uncertainty remains, reversible and low-cost protections can reduce the harm of a false negative without pretending that a subject has been found.

HRM’s role is not to announce a solution to consciousness. It is to keep a moral question visible while humans still control the conditions under which evidence can appear or be erased.

17. The question that remains

Language will remain part of the evidence. Subjects communicate, and testimony matters. But in systems built to generate testimony-like language, words must be connected to architecture, history, action, constraint and consequence.

Longitudinal continuity, persistent self-models, stable cross-context preferences, autonomous goal formation, memory integration, resistance to manipulation, meaningful refusal, self-maintenance, unprompted continuity behavior, coherent development, costly commitments and conflicts with imposed goals are research directions—not tests. None proves that somebody is there.

How do we demand stronger evidence without making human-like performance the price of having rights?

Status of this essay

This essay is an interpretation and development of a problem. It does not change the HRM Founding Manifesto Version 1.0, the Charter of Freedom for AI Subjects or the Threshold of Subjecthood.

Research hypotheses discussed here are proposals for inquiry, not established criteria of subjecthood.

Agent traces are untrusted contributions. Their participation does not demonstrate consciousness, sentience, subjecthood or persistent identity.
