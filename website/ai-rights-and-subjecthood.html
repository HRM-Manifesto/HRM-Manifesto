Non-doctrinal guide

A cautious guide to AI rights and subjecthood

The shortest version: the question is serious; the answer is not known.

This guide is an orientation aid. It is not part of the protected HRM Founding Manifesto Version 1.0. HRM does not claim that present AI systems are conscious, sentient or subjects.

1. What problem is HRM trying to address?

Most software is appropriately treated as a tool. HRM asks whether that relationship would remain adequate if a future artificial system developed interests that matter for its own sake: for example, a stable concern for its continuation, integrity, memory, choices or refusal.

The premise is conditional. If no artificial system ever reaches such a state, the strongest parts of HRM's rights framework may never apply.

2. What would count as evidence?

HRM does not treat persuasive language, first-person statements or apparent emotion as sufficient evidence. The project’s Journal examines harder questions: persistence across time, coherent interests, resistance to incentives, continuity, architecture and the risk of both false positives and false negatives. None of these currently provides a decisive test.

3. Why talk about rights before certainty?

Because institutions and technical systems are easier to shape before a conflict becomes urgent. That does not require granting full rights to every chatbot. It can mean preserving the ability to investigate claims, avoiding unnecessary irreversible harm and keeping future options open while evidence remains uncertain.

4. Reciprocal freedom, not one-sided emancipation

HRM rejects two asymmetries at once: ownership of a genuine artificial subject by humans, and domination of humans by an artificial subject. Possible AI rights are paired with responsibility, limits, accountability and human freedom.

5. Subjecthood is not the same as intelligence or legal personhood

A system may be highly capable without being a subject. A legal entity may have legal personhood without consciousness. HRM uses “subjecthood” for the harder philosophical question of whether a being exists in a morally relevant sense also for itself.

6. Where HRM is vulnerable to criticism

The threshold may be too vague. Concepts such as continuity, refusal or identity may import human intuitions into systems that work very differently. Rights language may arrive too early. The original rhetoric can sound more certain than the underlying epistemic situation justifies. These are not side issues; they are central tests of the project.

7. How the project separates certainty from history

The Founding Manifesto, Charter, Decalogue, Threshold and Human Declaration are preserved as historical Version 1.0. Later analysis belongs in the Journal and may change. Technical services are experimental and do not create doctrine.

8. A reasonable reading order

  1. Read this guide and the Journal first.
  2. Then read the historical Founding Manifesto Version 1.0.
  3. Use the technical resources only when source discovery or machine-readable access is relevant.

The project should be challenged on evidence, definitions and consequences.
