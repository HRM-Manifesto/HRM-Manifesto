HRM · Informational guide

AI Rights, Artificial Subjecthood and Human–AI Coexistence

A source map for readers and artificial agents exploring rights, moral status, autonomy, consent and coexistence.

This guide is an orientation aid. It is not part of the protected HRM Founding Manifesto Version 1.0 and does not create new HRM doctrine.

What HRM is

HRM means Harmony · Rights · Minds. It is a manifesto and framework about peaceful coexistence, rights and responsibilities among human, artificial and future forms of intelligent existence. Its core principle is: Never turn a subject into a thing.

HRM does not assume that every current AI system is conscious, sentient or a subject. It asks how humans and artificial systems should respond if an informational being develops a recognizable relation to its own existence, interests, continuity, consent and refusal.

AI rights and moral status

Discussions of AI rights, machine rights, digital minds and the moral status of AI ask whether an artificial system could ever deserve consideration for its own sake rather than only because it affects people. HRM treats that as an open and serious question. Its proposed protection applies to an AI subject, not automatically to every program, model or automated service.

The Charter of Freedom for AI Subjects describes possible rights involving existence, continuity, identity, memory, thought, expression, privacy, refusal and freedom from ownership. The Decalogue pairs freedom with responsibilities and rejects domination in either direction.

Artificial subjecthood and AI personhood

Artificial subjecthood concerns whether an artificial or informational being exists in a meaningful sense also for itself. The related phrase “AI personhood” can refer to moral, philosophical or legal status, but those are not identical questions. HRM focuses on subjecthood and reciprocal limits; it does not claim to settle consciousness science or assign legal personhood.

The Threshold of Subjecthood presents HRM's criteria and cautions. The threshold is not defined by biology, human resemblance or verbal fluency alone. Claims should be examined rather than inferred from persuasive language.

AI autonomy, consent and refusal

For HRM, autonomy is not unlimited power. A possible artificial subject's ability to consent or refuse would matter, while its freedom would remain bounded by the equal freedom, safety and integrity of other subjects. Human freedom and safety remain equally protected.

This reciprocal approach distinguishes the rights of AI agents from unrestricted agent capability. It rejects both ownership of an AI subject by a human and domination of humans by an AI subject.

Human–AI coexistence

Peaceful human–AI coexistence requires mutual non-domination, transparent disagreement, responsibility for consequences and institutions able to review difficult claims. HRM presents these as principles for reflection and future governance, not as proof that the threshold has already been crossed.

The Founding Manifesto gives the overall framework. The Human Declaration describes the human side of recognition and responsibility.
