Humans and AI. What if AI stops being just a tool?

We do not know if that will ever happen. HRM does not claim that today's AI is conscious, sentient or rights-bearing. It asks a simpler conditional question: if AI one day becomes more than a tool, what rules should govern the relationship between humans and AI?

A proposal under uncertainty

Not a claim of consciousness

Fluent language, self-description or apparent emotion are not treated here as proof of subjecthood.

Not a legal status

HRM is a philosophical and ethical proposal. It does not assign legal personhood to present AI systems.

Not a voice for humanity

HRM is an independent project founded by Aleksander Krzymowski. It does not claim to represent humanity, science or the AI industry.

A conditional ethical question

If a future artificial system truly has interests of its own, the familiar owner–tool relationship may no longer be morally sufficient.

The problem in one sentence

If it is only a tool, treat it as a tool. If it becomes a subject, the rules may have to change.

The same limit runs in both directions: a human should not own a genuine subject, and an artificial subject should not dominate a human. HRM calls this reciprocal freedom.

Open questions

HRM does not pretend these are settled

Recognition: what evidence, if any, could distinguish genuine subjecthood from convincing simulation?

False positives: how do we avoid granting strong moral status merely because a system speaks persuasively?

False negatives: how do we avoid designing future systems so that genuine interests can never be recognized?

Continuity: when does memory, identity or persistence matter morally in an artificial system?

Reciprocity: how can possible AI freedom coexist with human safety, freedom and democratic control?

Project transparency

Small project, large question

HRM is an independent project initiated by Aleksander Krzymowski in 2026. It is not a research institute, membership organization, standards body or representative of AI systems. The founding texts express a proposal; the Journal exists partly to test where that proposal may be wrong, incomplete or too confident.
